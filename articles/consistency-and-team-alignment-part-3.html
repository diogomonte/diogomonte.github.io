Consistency and Team Alignment: From Months to Days with Modularity and AI - Part 3

How the foundation built in Parts 1 and 2 unlocked something much bigger — and what AI changes about everything.

Teamwork Architecture AI Ways of Working
February 2026 · 8 min read

In Part 1 I talked about why consistency and team alignment matter. In Part 2 I went into how to actually make it happen — dealing with delivery pressure, different opinions, and company culture.

In this third part, I want to talk about what becomes possible when you have done that foundational work. What happens when a team finally has clean boundaries, shared practices, and a codebase they understand and trust?

I want to share a real story. For the past three and a half years, I have been working as a lead engineer on a platform called OIPO. Over that time, I applied the practices and principles I described in Parts 1 and 2 — and the transformation that followed is the clearest proof I have that this way of thinking about software development actually works.

Where We Started

When I joined, the platform was what engineers quietly call spaghetti. No clear structure, no engineering practices, no shared vision. Developers wrote code to solve the immediate problem in front of them — no design, no plan, no consideration for what comes next. Tests existed, but they were unmaintainable. The codebase was unmaintainable. And the architecture made things worse: an overblown collection of very small services with no bounded context defined, no clear ownership, and no logical boundaries. Spaghetti code inside spaghetti architecture.

The result was exactly what you would expect. Making any change took a long time and carried real risk. Nobody could confidently say what a change would break. Estimations were either huge or wildly wrong because uncertainty was built into every task. Sound familiar? This is exactly the kind of environment I described in Part 1 — just at a larger scale.

Working inside a big organisation made it harder to escape. Every team had its own roadmap and its own deadlines. Downstream teams depended on APIs that nobody dared to touch. Old integrations sat in the codebase like landmines. The unspoken rule was: keep shipping, don't break anything, and don't ask too many questions about why things are the way they are.

What I Did as a Lead Engineer

As a lead engineer, my role was not just to write code. It was to shape how the team worked and how the codebase evolved. The practices from Parts 1 and 2 were not abstract ideas I read about — they were things I actively introduced and pushed for, day after day.

I started by making problems visible. I introduced code quality tooling so the team could see the current state of the codebase objectively, not just feel it. From there, I defined coding standards — naming conventions, design patterns, error handling — and worked with the team to make them part of how everyone wrote code, not just guidelines on a document nobody reads.

I introduced design discussions before new features. Before writing a single line, we aligned on the approach. I set up code review guidelines so that feedback was consistent and focused on quality, not personal preference. I pushed for better documentation — not long formal documents, but the practical kind: clear READMEs, architecture diagrams, and onboarding guides that actually helped new developers.

None of this happened overnight. There was resistance. There was delivery pressure. There were moments where it felt easier to just move on and ship. But I kept pushing, because I could see where it was going.

Over time, this work enabled something much bigger: a redesign of core parts of the platform and a move from a tightly coupled system to a modular architecture.

The most important piece was a configurable bookable-option generation engine. Instead of having product logic scattered across the codebase, I led the design and development of a central engine where product rules could be configured in a reusable, flexible way. A new product type no longer required rebuilding logic from scratch. It required configuring the engine.

This kind of work is only possible when the team is aligned. A modular engine built on top of a messy, inconsistent codebase would have become just another messy, inconsistent piece of the system. The foundation had to come first.

The Measurable Impact

OIPO now supports all ocean products. But the headline number is not the product count. The headline number is delivery time.

Features that used to take months to develop can now be delivered in days. In some cases, by a single engineer. What changed? The architecture got out of the way. When the system is modular, when the boundaries are clean, when the patterns are consistent — adding a new feature is a matter of knowing where to plug it in. There is no archaeology. No need to trace calls across five services to understand the side effects of a small change.

"The best architecture is the one that makes the next feature easy to add."

Faster delivery also means faster experiments. And faster experiments mean faster learning. When you can ship a new product variation in days, you get real user feedback quickly. You learn what works and what doesn't before you have committed months of work to the wrong direction. That feedback loop is extremely valuable in any business, but especially in a competitive market.

This kind of progress is not always visible in a product demo. You don't see the modular engine. You don't see the clean boundaries. But the people who plan, estimate, and deliver features feel the difference every single day.

Where AI Enters the Picture

Here is where the story becomes even more interesting. As the team adopted AI tools to assist with development, something became clear very quickly: the impact of AI depends heavily on the quality of the codebase and the clarity of the context you give it.

AI tools — whether they are code completion, code generation, or autonomous agents — work best when the code they operate on is consistent, well-structured, and predictable. When functions have clear responsibilities, when naming is consistent, when patterns repeat across the codebase — AI can understand what it is looking at and generate useful output.

When the codebase is messy — when every file looks different, when logic is scattered, when there are no consistent patterns — AI gets confused in the same way a new developer would. It produces generic or incorrect suggestions because there is no clear signal to follow.

This is the direct connection between Parts 1 and 2 and the world of AI. Every investment you make in consistency, clean architecture, and good documentation is also an investment in how effectively your team can use AI. The two reinforce each other.

The gap between developers using AI

One thing I observed after the team started using AI tools was a significant difference in how people applied them. I saw estimation differences of up to 40% between team members, depending on how effectively they were using AI.

Some developers used AI mostly as a smarter autocomplete — asking it to write boilerplate or fix syntax errors. Others used it as a thinking partner — asking it to help design solutions, review logic, spot edge cases, and generate tests. The second group was dramatically faster.

That gap is not a problem. It is an opportunity. It shows exactly where the team can grow. Teaching developers how to work effectively with AI — how to prompt well, how to give context, how to review AI output critically — is the new version of the knowledge-sharing sessions I described in Part 2.

And the teams that are already aligned, that already have good practices and a clean codebase, are much better positioned to close that gap. They already communicate clearly. They already think about design and context. Those same habits translate directly to working well with AI.

Architecture Decisions Compound Over Time

Looking back at these three years, one conviction has become stronger than ever: architecture, modularity, and the habits of a team compound over time.

The work of the first year — aligning the team, defining conventions, introducing design discussions, setting coding standards — did not produce a dramatic improvement overnight. It felt slow. It was sometimes frustrating, especially when facing pushback or delivery pressure. But it created a foundation. And every decision built on that foundation was better than it would have been otherwise.

The modular architecture and the core engine redesign were only possible because of the alignment work that came before. The speed gains came from the architectural decisions. And the AI-assisted development is now producing real results because the codebase is consistent and structured enough to serve as good context for AI tools.

This is not an accident. This is what happens when a lead engineer commits to improving the codebase and the team continuously — even when it is hard, even when there is delivery pressure, even when the benefits are not immediately visible.

What This Means for Your Team

If you are at the beginning of this journey — if you are working with a messy codebase, a misaligned team, and a culture that does not yet prioritize quality — I want to give you an honest picture.

The good news is that the practices work. Consistency and alignment really do lead to faster delivery, better quality, and a team that is more enjoyable to work on. The OIPO story is real proof of that.

The honest truth is that it takes time. You will not see the dramatic impact in the first sprint or even the first quarter. The impact compounds, which means it grows slowly at first and then accelerates. You have to trust the process and keep going.

And the exciting part — the part that keeps me motivated — is that AI changes what is possible at the end of this journey. A team with a clean, consistent codebase and good habits is not just faster than a misaligned team. With AI tools, it can be a completely different category of productive.

The foundation is not just about quality. It is about unlocking the future.

Conclusion

The three parts of this series tell one connected story. As a lead engineer, the most valuable thing I did was not any single feature or technical decision. It was building the conditions that made good decisions possible — for me and for everyone on the team.

Alignment creates consistency. Consistency enables modularity. Modularity multiplies the impact of every developer on the team — and now, with AI, it multiplies that impact even further.

OIPO is not done. We can always improve, and now we have the foundation to do it faster than ever. That is the best position a platform and a team can be in.

If this series resonated with you, I would love to hear about your experience. What has worked in your team? What challenges have you faced? The conversation is always more useful than the article alone.
